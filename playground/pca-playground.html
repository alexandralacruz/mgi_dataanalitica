// THE CONCEPT

Variance measures how spread out your data is from its center (the mean). It answers: "on average, how far are the data points from the middle?"

σ² = (1 / (n-1)) Σ (xᵢ - x̄)²
  • x̄ = mean (average of all points)
  • (xᵢ - x̄) = deviation from mean
  • ( )² = squared so negatives don't cancel out
  • Σ / (n-1) = average it out (Bessel's correction)
// ANALOGY: THE CLASSROOM

Imagine students standing in a line. The mean is where the teacher stands (the center). Variance is how spread out the students are. If everyone crowds the teacher, variance is low. If students scatter across the room, variance is high.

// WHY SQUARED DISTANCES?

The red lines show deviations from the mean. If we just summed them, positive and negative would cancel to zero! Squaring makes all distances positive and penalizes big outliers more than small ones.

// WHY PCA CARES ABOUT VARIANCE

PCA finds the direction of maximum variance. Why? Because variance = information. A direction with high variance means data points differ a lot along it — that's where the interesting structure is.

  • High variance direction = lots of information, keep it
  • Low variance direction = mostly noise, safe to drop
  • Eigenvalue = the variance along each principal component
// ANALOGY: SHADOWS

Imagine shining a flashlight on a 3D object. The shadow that captures the most detail (widest spread) is like PC1 — it preserves the most variance. PCA finds the best angle for the flashlight.

// HOW PCA WORKS - STEP BY STEP

01
Standardize the data
X_std = (X - mean(X)) / std(X)
02
Compute covariance matrix
Sigma = (1/(m-1)) X_std^T X_std
03
Eigen decomposition
Sigma v_i = lambda_i v_i
04
Sort & select components
W = [v_1, v_2, ..., v_k] (sorted by lambda desc)
05
Project data
X_reduced = X_std @ W
