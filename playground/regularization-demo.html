Why Do We Need Regularization?

When training a model, we want it to generalize — perform well on new, unseen data, not just memorize the training set. Without regularization, models with enough capacity will overfit: they fit the noise in the data rather than the underlying pattern.

What is happening?

Key insight: As degree increases, the model fits training data better (lower training error) but eventually fits the noise, causing test error to rise.

The Bias-Variance Tradeoff

  • High bias (underfitting): Model too simple, can't capture the pattern
  • High variance (overfitting): Model too complex, captures noise
  • Sweet spot: Enough complexity to capture the pattern, not enough to fit noise

The Regularization Solution

Regularization adds a penalty term to the cost function that discourages the model from becoming too complex — it "regularizes" (constrains) the weights.

J(w, b) = 1/(2m) Σ (ŷ - y)² + λ/(2m) Σ wⱼ²
■ data loss   ■ regularization penalty
λ (lambda) controls the strength of regularization:
• λ = 0 → no regularization (risk of overfitting)
• λ too large → underfitting (weights shrink to ~0)
• λ just right → good generalization

Key Differences

L2 (Ridge): J + λ Σ wⱼ²
L1 (Lasso): J + λ Σ |wⱼ|
L2 (Ridge) L1 (Lasso)
PenaltySum of squaresSum of absolutes
Constraint shapeCircleDiamond
Effect on weightsShrinks allShrinks some to zero
Feature selection?NoYes
Best whenAll features matterFew features matter
Why does L1 produce zeros? The diamond shape of L1's constraint region has corners that sit on the axes. The cost function contours are more likely to first touch these corners — setting one weight exactly to zero. This effectively removes that feature!
Elastic Net combines both: λ₁ Σ|wⱼ| + λ₂ Σwⱼ² — giving you feature selection and smooth shrinkage.
